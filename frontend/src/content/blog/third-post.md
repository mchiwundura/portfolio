## Introduction

Health technology carries enormous promise. From mobile diagnostics and AI-driven triage tools to digital supply chains and telemedicine platforms, the prospect of extending quality healthcare to millions of underserved people is both real and urgent. In a continent where a nurse in a rural clinic may serve thousands of patients with little more than a paper register and intermittent electricity, the right digital tool could genuinely save lives.

Yet despite this promise, the history of health tech in low-resource settings is littered with pilots that never scaled, platforms that sat unused, and systems that collapsed the moment donor funding dried up. The failure is rarely technological — the apps work, the algorithms are sound, the hardware is functional. The failure is contextual. Solutions built without a deep understanding of the economic, social, infrastructural, and political environments they enter are solutions that will not survive contact with those environments.

Understanding these barriers is not a pessimistic exercise. It is the foundation of building solutions that actually work. This article maps those barriers honestly, explores the real opportunities that health technology can unlock, and offers design principles drawn from hard-won field experience across the continent.

## Part One: The Barriers

#### 1.1 Poverty: The Elephant in the Room

No conversation about health technology in Africa is complete without confronting poverty first. Implementing AI-driven diagnostics, IoT-connected clinics, cloud infrastructure, or blockchain-based health records requires meaningful financial investment in hardware, connectivity, training, maintenance, and ongoing support. When large portions of a population are focused on meeting basic daily needs, that investment is not realistic at either the individual or institutional level.

Even where technology is available and affordable by global standards, the cost of a smartphone, a mobile data subscription, or access to a digital health platform can be prohibitive. In clinical settings, the calculation is often starker still: when resources are scarce, medication comes before apps. This is not irrationality — it is triage.

The result is that the benefits of digital health innovations tend to concentrate among a small, already-advantaged fraction of the population. It is telling that the countries leading the continent's digital health transformation — South Africa, Kenya, Nigeria, and Ghana — are also, by African standards, its conventionally wealthier economies. Wealth creates the conditions for adoption; poverty forecloses them.

#### 1.2 Under-resourced Clinic Infrastructure

Even where funding exists, physical infrastructure presents its own formidable barriers. Healthcare facilities across low-resource settings routinely contend with unreliable electricity supplies, poor road networks that limit supply chains and patient access, and a chronic shortage of computers and digital equipment. Without these foundations, sophisticated digital tools cannot operate reliably, however elegant their design.

Connectivity is a particular challenge. Many African countries still experience limited internet penetration, especially outside major urban centres. Slow networks, expensive mobile data, and patchy rural coverage severely constrain the effectiveness of any platform that depends on consistent internet access. The picture is improving — mobile telecommunications have advanced significantly in recent years alongside satellite internet constellations the likes of Starlink — but the gap between urban and rural connectivity remains wide.

> **Real-world example:** Across multiple mHealth pilots in sub-Saharan Africa, community health workers used digital tools that functioned offline but required network access to upload data and generate real-time reports. Frequent power outages and connectivity dropouts meant critical health decisions were regularly delayed — defeating the primary purpose of the tools. _(PMC, 2022)_

Similarly, efforts to transition to electronic health records in rural settings have repeatedly stalled due to inadequate electricity, unreliable internet, and the absence of legal frameworks to govern digital data. Successful pilots rarely transitioned into national systems. Paper records remain dominant.

#### 1.3 The Digital Skills Gap

Deploying health technology is not the same as adopting it. Healthcare workers must understand how to use digital tools effectively, integrate them into clinical workflows, and troubleshoot basic problems without external support. This requires a level of digital literacy that many health systems — particularly those operating in low-resource environments — have not yet been able to build.

The roots of this gap run deep. Educational systems across much of the continent have not yet adapted their curricula to prepare the workforce for modern digital healthcare environments. The result is a generation of skilled, dedicated health professionals who are nonetheless poorly equipped to navigate the digital tools increasingly being thrust upon them.

In Nigeria, for instance, hospital-based electronic health record pilots faced strong resistance from clinical staff who experienced the systems as additional administrative burden rather than support. Low adoption rates, compounded by fragmented data systems and insufficient training, prevented these pilots from achieving meaningful scale. The technology was functional; the human infrastructure was not ready.

Even in environments where digital systems are technically adopted, the depth of their use can remain extremely limited. In many hospitals and clinics, healthcare professionals interact with electronic systems only at the most basic level — entering minimal patient information such as a patient's name, surname, date of birth, and prescribed medications while omitting richer clinical information like diagnostic notes, laboratory findings, imaging reports, or treatment outcomes. This pattern often arises from time pressure, unfamiliarity with the systems, or the perception that detailed data entry creates additional administrative work with little immediate clinical benefit. As a result, electronic health record systems that were intended to create comprehensive patient histories frequently end up functioning as little more than digital registration tools. When clinical data is incomplete, the broader benefits of digital health systems — such as disease surveillance, healthcare analytics, and medical research — are significantly weakened.

Zimbabwe is beginning to move in a more deliberate direction. The country's National AI Strategy 2026–2030 sets out a framework for AI literacy and workforce development, and initiatives like the World Robot Olympiad Zimbabwe 2025 — bringing together students, educators, Econet, and the Ministry of Education — represent early investments in building a digitally capable generation. These are steps forward, but closing the skills gap in healthcare specifically will require targeted, sustained investment.

#### 1.4 Cultural Resistance and the Weight of Established Practice

Healthcare systems are built on trust — trust in practitioners, in methods, in institutions. Many systems rely heavily on experienced professionals who have spent careers building that trust through established workflows and time-tested practices. When digital tools arrive and appear to disrupt or complicate those workflows, resistance is a rational response, not mere stubbornness.

This resistance takes different forms. At the professional level, it is often about perceived workload and competence: digital systems can make experienced practitioners feel deskilled or burdened. At the community level, it runs deeper. Many communities have strong trust in traditional medicine and established treatment methods. When a digital health system appears to dismiss or undermine local knowledge — or when it is perceived as an external imposition with little understanding of local context — communities may simply reject it.

Successful health tech solutions must take this seriously. Respecting existing practices, engaging communities as co-designers rather than passive recipients, and demonstrating tangible benefit before demanding behaviour change are not soft considerations — they are preconditions for adoption. Technology that attempts abrupt disruption, without earning trust, will fail.

#### 1.5 Governance, Politics, and Regulatory Complexity

Regulatory frameworks across much of Africa have not kept pace with the speed of digital health innovation. Bureaucratic approval processes, unclear or absent digital health policies, inconsistent enforcement, and — critically — weak data governance regimes create an environment where even well-resourced innovators struggle to operate with confidence.

Data sovereignty is not a theoretical concern. Zimbabwe's 2023 decision to decline a significant healthcare technology agreement with the United States — citing concerns about sovereignty and the privacy of medical data — illustrates how deeply governments feel the stakes. Health data is among the most sensitive data a person generates. When communities and governments do not trust that their data will be protected and kept under their control, they will resist digital health systems, often rightly.

#### 1.6 Cybersecurity and Data Safety

This connects directly to cybersecurity. Many low-resource environments lack the infrastructure and expertise to defend digital health systems against breaches or infrastructure attacks. Weak security does not just expose patients — it corrodes the public trust that health systems depend on. It also feeds the instinct toward data localisation policies that further restrict the use of cloud computing for health data, creating a difficult tension for organisations trying to build scalable, affordable solutions on cloud infrastructure.

#### 1.7 Market Fragmentation and Investment Risk

Africa is not a single market. It is fifty-four countries with distinct regulatory environments, infrastructure realities, languages, and healthcare systems. A platform built, tested, and refined for Zimbabwe may face significant re-engineering to operate in Zambia, let alone Senegal. This fragmentation creates real risks for health tech companies attempting to scale: the investment required to adapt solutions across markets can make the economics deeply unattractive.

Regional integration — the kind that creates larger, more harmonised digital health markets — would make health technology investment more viable. Without it, the continent will continue to attract pilots and proof-of-concepts rather than the sustained, scaling investments that actually change healthcare at population level.

#### 1.8 Building Solutions for the Wrong Problem

There is a particular failure mode worth naming directly: the innovator who sees a sophisticated health technology showcased in the United States or Europe and decides to replicate it for a context in which the foundational prerequisites — reliable power, internet connectivity, trained operators, maintained equipment — do not exist. The innovation may be technically impressive. It may even work in controlled conditions. But it is solving the wrong problem.

Low-resource health systems often need better basics before they need advanced digital tools: reliable patient record systems, functional supply chain management, accurate disease reporting. Solutions that address these foundational needs, and that are designed for the actual infrastructure and skill levels present in target settings, will outperform technically sophisticated solutions that assume conditions that do not exist.

#### 1.9 Distrust of Digital Systems

Finally, there is a legitimate wariness toward digital health systems that is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing. Software bugs in consumer applications cause inconvenience. In clinical contexts, they can contribute to patient harm. When digital tools have failed publicly or produced errors in high-stakes situations, communities and clinicians remember. That memory creates a scepticism that solution designers must earn their way past — through rigorous testing, transparent error-handling, and demonstrated reliability over time.

## Part Two: The Opportunities

Despite these challenges, health technology offers genuine and significant opportunities — provided it is designed and deployed with realism about the context it is entering.

#### 2.1 Reducing Youth Unemployment

Health technology can create new career pathways for young people that do not require traditional medical degrees. The growing ecosystem of digital health creates demand for health data analysts, digital health technicians, medical software developers, telemedicine coordinators, and community health informatics workers. In a continent with a rapidly growing youth population and stubbornly high youth unemployment, this matters.

#### 2.2 Improving Healthcare System Efficiency

Digital systems can dramatically improve patient record management, treatment coordination, supply chain monitoring, and healthcare accessibility — particularly across geographically dispersed populations. By reducing duplication, error, and inefficiency, well-designed digital health systems can lower the overall cost of care delivery, freeing resources for frontline services.

#### 2.3 Reducing Corruption

Technologies such as blockchain and tamper-resistant digital records have genuine potential to reduce corruption in healthcare systems by making medical records, procurement transactions, and drug supply chains more transparent and auditable. In settings where healthcare resources are sometimes diverted through opaque manual processes, this transparency has real value.

#### 2.4 Improving Health Education

Mobile health platforms can put reliable health information in the hands of communities that have historically had limited access to it — improving disease prevention, maternal health, vaccination uptake, and public health awareness at scale.

#### 2.5 Early Outbreak Detection and Pharmacovigilance

Digital surveillance systems can detect patterns in disease spread significantly earlier than traditional reporting chains allow. Faster detection means faster response — a distinction that matters enormously when an outbreak is accelerating.

Equally important is the strengthening of pharmacovigilance systems. Monitoring the safety and effectiveness of medicines in real-world use is essential for protecting patients and improving treatment guidelines. Digital pharmacovigilance platforms can enable faster reporting of adverse drug reactions, better aggregation of data across health facilities, and earlier identification of safety signals that might otherwise remain unnoticed. In low-resource settings, where reporting is often fragmented or paper-based, digitizing pharmacovigilance workflows can greatly improve the quality, timeliness, and completeness of drug safety data, ultimately supporting safer prescribing practices and more responsive regulatory oversight.

#### 2.6 Medicine Demand Forecasting

Health data can be used to predict medication demand with far greater accuracy than current manual forecasting methods, preventing drug shortages and reducing the waste that comes from overstocking. This has direct implications for supply chain efficiency and patient outcomes.

#### 2.7 Advancing Medical Research

Large, well-structured digital health datasets are a significant research asset. Better records of patient outcomes, treatment responses, and disease progression can help researchers study how medicines perform in real African populations — data that is currently severely underrepresented in global medical literature. Digital records could also enable more rigorous scientific study of traditional medicines, integrating indigenous knowledge with evidence-based frameworks.

#### 2.8 Personalized Medicine and Long-Term Cost Reduction

As data infrastructure matures, the possibility of personalized medicine — treatment plans tailored to individual patient profiles rather than population averages — becomes more realistic. Equally, the preventive and efficiency gains from well-implemented digital health systems compound over time, reducing long-term healthcare costs significantly.

## Part Three: Principles for Building Health Tech That Works

For health technology to succeed in low-resource settings, it must be designed with a clear-eyed understanding of those settings.

#### 3.1 Design for the Infrastructure That Exists, Not the Infrastructure You Wish Existed

Many users rely on affordable Android smartphones operating on intermittent data connections. Health tech solutions should prioritise low data consumption, offline-first functionality with reliable synchronisation when connectivity is available, and compatibility with older, lower-specification devices. Mobile telecommunications have made significant advances, but an offline-first architecture remains essential for now — and for much of the continent, for the foreseeable future.

This principle extends to power: solutions that assume reliable electricity will fail in settings where power is intermittent. Design around that reality.

#### 3.2 Invest Seriously in Training and Onboarding

Healthcare workers must receive clear, practical, ongoing training when new technologies are introduced. Systems should be simple enough to learn quickly, integrated into existing workflows rather than added on top of them, and supported by local champions who can provide peer-level assistance. One-off training sessions at launch are not sufficient. Embedding digital skills development into institutional practice is.

#### 3.3 Invest in Cybersecurity and Data Governance

Organisations must treat cybersecurity not as an afterthought but as a core design requirement. This means implementing internationally recognised security standards, investing in threat detection, training staff on data handling practices, and — critically — ensuring that data governance frameworks respect the sovereignty concerns of governments and the privacy expectations of patients. Trust, once lost, is very difficult to rebuild.

#### 3.4 Build Skills at the National Level

Governments and development partners should increase investment in upskilling programs focused on AI, data analytics, and digital health management. Partnerships between universities, private sector organisations, and technology companies can accelerate this work. Zimbabwe's National AI Strategy 2026–2030 represents the kind of structural commitment that, if implemented with genuine follow-through, could begin to close the skills gap over a generation.

#### 3.5 Respect Culture — and Design for It

Successful adoption requires earning trust, not demanding compliance. Change management must begin before technology is deployed: engaging clinical leaders, community representatives, and frontline workers in the design process creates ownership and surfaces the cultural and workflow realities that will determine whether a tool is used. Solutions that respect existing practices and integrate traditional knowledge rather than dismissing it will find far more receptive communities.

#### 3.6 Design for Simplicity

Complexity is the enemy of adoption. User interfaces must be intuitive, accessible, and require minimal steps to complete common tasks. Every additional click or screen is a potential dropout point. The measure of good design in this context is not technical sophistication — it is the degree to which technology supports healthcare delivery without demanding that practitioners think about the technology itself.

#### 3.7 Build for Sustainability from the Start

Solutions dependent on continuous donor funding or external technical support will not survive the end of the grant cycle. Viable health tech in low-resource settings must have a credible path to financial sustainability — whether through government adoption, insurance integration, appropriately structured user fees, or commercial models. Sustainability planning must begin at conception, not at scale-up.

## Conclusion

The failure of health technology initiatives in low-resource settings is rarely the failure of technology. It is the failure of context — of solutions that did not account for poverty, infrastructure, governance, culture, or the human realities of the environments they entered.

The opportunity remains real and large. Digital tools, well-designed and thoughtfully deployed, can extend healthcare to underserved populations, reduce systemic inefficiencies, create jobs, and advance research in ways that are genuinely transformative. But transformation requires honesty about the terrain. Solutions that prioritise accessibility, simplicity, local ownership, and sustainable models have the best chance of creating meaningful improvements in healthcare systems. Solutions that do not will add to the long list of promising pilots that never became anything more.

Building health technology that works is, ultimately, less a technical challenge than a human one. Getting it right demands listening before building, and humility before innovation.
